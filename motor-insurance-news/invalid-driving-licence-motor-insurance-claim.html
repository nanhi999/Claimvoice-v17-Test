Invalid Driving Licence & Motor Insurance Claim: Can Insurer Reject It?

Consumer Commission directs insurer to pay ₹3.5 lakh after a motor claim was rejected over a driving-licence issue.

A motor insurance claim should not necessarily be treated as invalid merely because an insurer raises an objection about the driver's driving licence.

A recent case reported by The Economic Times highlights an important point for vehicle owners: an alleged invalid, fake or defective driving licence does not automatically give an insurer a free pass to reject a motor insurance claim.

In an order dated 8 July 2026, the West Bengal State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission directed HDFC General Insurance Company to pay ₹3.5 lakh with interest to the policyholder and also awarded ₹1 lakh as compensation for mental harassment. The Commission found that the insurer had not produced sufficient evidence to establish the required breach by the insured.

What happened in the case?

The case involved a Skoda New Laura Ambiente 1.8 TSI insured for an IDV of about ₹6.95 lakh. The policy was valid from 9 March 2014 to 9 March 2015.

The car met with an accident on 12 October 2014. It was taken to an authorised Skoda service centre, where the estimated repair cost was about ₹5.28 lakh.

The insurer rejected the claim after questioning the validity of the driving licence of the person driving the vehicle. The insurer alleged that the driver had another licence, including an allegedly fake licence issued in Nagaland, and relied on the restriction against holding more than one driving licence.

Did holding two licences automatically end the policyholder's claim?

No. The Commission distinguished between a driver's alleged violation of the Motor Vehicles Act and a breach of the insurance policy by the vehicle owner.

In other words, even if a driver has violated a statutory provision relating to driving licences, that does not by itself prove that the insured deliberately breached the insurance policy in a way that permits the insurer to avoid its contractual liability.

The Commission relied on Supreme Court principles discussed in cases including National Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Swaran Singh and Nirmala Kothari v. United India Insurance Co. Ltd.

Does an invalid or fake driving licence automatically justify rejection?

Not necessarily.

The important distinction is between a defect in the driver's licence and a proven breach of the insurance policy by the insured.

The reported decision emphasises that the insurer must establish the relevant breach. The circumstances can include whether the vehicle owner exercised reasonable care while appointing or allowing the driver to drive, what the owner knew or should reasonably have known, and whether the alleged breach has the legal significance required to defeat the claim.

What is "reasonable care" by a vehicle owner?

A vehicle owner who employs or permits a driver to use the insured vehicle should take reasonable steps to ensure that the person has an appropriate driving licence.

Keeping a copy of the driver's licence and relevant employment or verification records can be useful if a claim is later disputed.

However, as reflected in the legal principles discussed in the reported case, a vehicle owner is not necessarily expected to independently investigate every apparently genuine licence with transport authorities across the country unless circumstances give reason to suspect that the licence is not genuine.

What did the Commission consider about the accident and surveyor's assessment?

The reported decision also considered the assessment of vehicle damage. The Commission questioned aspects of the surveyor's calculation, including depreciation applied to certain parts and the basis for labour-charge calculations.

The report also notes the surveyor's account that the tyre became stuck in a tram line and the car then hit a road divider. The Commission considered the alleged licence issue separately from the immediate circumstances of the accident and did not accept that the technical licence objection, on the evidence presented, was enough to defeat the indemnity.

How much was the insurer ordered to pay?

  • ₹3.5 lakh to the policyholder, with interest at 6% per annum from 7 September 2016, payable within 45 days.
  • If the amount was not paid within the prescribed period, the order provided for continued interest at 6% up to the date of the order and thereafter at 9% per annum until final payment.
  • ₹1 lakh as compensation for mental harassment.

The Commission did not award the full ₹6.95 lakh IDV claimed by the policyholder.

What does this mean for ordinary car owners?

The case should not be misunderstood as saying that an insurer must pay every claim involving an invalid licence.

Nor should it be understood to mean that a licence objection can always be ignored.

The practical lesson is that the exact licence problem, policy wording, evidence, owner's conduct, investigation findings and circumstances of the accident all matter.

When can a driving-licence issue become serious?

Every case is fact-specific. The position can be substantially different where:

  • the driver had no driving licence at all;
  • the licence had expired;
  • the licence did not authorise the relevant class of vehicle;
  • the owner knowingly permitted an unlicensed person to drive;
  • the owner ignored obvious warning signs about the licence; or
  • the insurer can establish the policy breach required by the applicable law and policy terms.

Therefore, the safest approach is not to assume either that an invalid licence always defeats a claim or that it never does.

What should you do if your motor claim is rejected because of a driving licence?

1. Obtain the written repudiation letter

Do not rely only on a telephone call, surveyor's statement or verbal explanation. Ask the insurer for the formal written rejection or repudiation letter.

2. Identify the exact allegation

Check whether the insurer is alleging no licence, an expired licence, a fake licence, a duplicate licence, an incorrect vehicle category, suspension or another specific defect.

3. Ask what evidence the insurer relied upon

If the insurer relies on an investigation report, RTO verification or other material, request the documents or findings relied upon for the rejection, subject to applicable disclosure rules.

4. Preserve your own evidence

  • Driver's licence and available verification records
  • RC and insurance policy
  • Claim form and claim-intimation record
  • FIR and accident documents, where applicable
  • Photographs and repair estimate
  • Survey report and correspondence
  • Driver employment or authorisation records, where applicable

5. Challenge an incorrect repudiation

If the insurer has rejected the claim without properly establishing the alleged breach, use the insurer's grievance mechanism and consider the appropriate external dispute-resolution or legal remedy based on the facts of the case.

The biggest lesson

A motor insurance claim should be examined on the basis of the actual policy terms, evidence and circumstances, rather than accepting a rejection label at face value.

If the insurer says "invalid licence — claim rejected", ask:

  • What exactly is invalid?
  • What evidence proves it?
  • What policy condition was allegedly breached?
  • What did the vehicle owner know?
  • Was reasonable care exercised?
  • What was the connection, if any, between the alleged breach and the accident?

These questions can make a significant difference when a motor insurance claim is disputed.

ClaimVoice Takeaway

An insurer raising a driving-licence objection does not automatically mean that the policyholder has no remedy.

At the same time, this case does not mean that every claim involving an invalid or fake licence must be paid. Each dispute must be examined on its own facts, policy wording and evidence.
