CLAIMVOICE • DOCUMENTS & EVIDENCE LIBRARY

Know what to keep.
Know what it proves.

A motor claim is not only a form. It is a record of what happened. This library explains what common documents are for, what to check before submitting them, what evidence can strengthen your file, and what information you should never share casually.

⌕
The evidence rule

Do not collect documents merely because someone says “submit everything.” Collect records that help establish what happened, when it happened, what was damaged, who had an interest in the vehicle, what was reported, what was repaired, and what amount was claimed.

01 • FIRST EVIDENCE

Photographs and video: create a record before the scene changes

Photos can preserve details that disappear after towing, washing, dismantling or repair. They are especially useful when the extent, location or nature of damage later becomes disputed.

Capture where practical

  • Whole vehicle from several angles.
  • Close-ups of every damaged area.
  • Registration number/vehicle identity.
  • Surrounding scene where relevant.
  • Road, weather, obstruction or other relevant context.
  • Parts or contents affected in theft/fire cases.

Example

A rear panel is repaired and later the parties disagree about whether a nearby bumper mark was part of the same loss. A dated set of wider and close-up photographs from before repair may help establish the condition.

Good practice: Keep the original files where possible. Do not edit, crop away relevant information or manufacture evidence. Back up important photographs.

The claim number is the reference that ties later communication to the file. Record the date and time of intimation, method used, person/channel contacted, and the information supplied.

03 • COVERAGE

Policy schedule: start here before arguing about coverage

The policy schedule can tell you which vehicle is insured, the policy period, IDV, type of cover, deductibles and selected add-ons. The full wording is equally important because the schedule alone does not contain every condition and exclusion.

Check these together: Policy schedule + policy wording + endorsements + add-on wording + claim facts. Keep the version applicable to the incident.
04 • VEHICLE IDENTITY

RC and vehicle documents

Registration records help establish the vehicle's identity and registered details. Keep a clear copy of the relevant registration document and check that the registration number, make/model and other important particulars are consistent with the policy.

Example: If the policy shows one registration number and a document shows another, do not ignore the mismatch. Find out whether it is a clerical issue, a transfer/update issue or something requiring an endorsement.
05 • DRIVER

Driving licence

Where the circumstances make the driver's licence relevant, preserve the licence details as they existed at the time of the incident. Do not alter dates, categories or images.

Record

Driver's identity, licence number, validity, relevant vehicle category and the date/time of the incident.

Privacy: Do not post a full licence publicly on a website, social media or an open forum. Share sensitive documents only through an appropriate official channel when legitimately required.
06 • POLICE RECORDS

FIR, police complaint, DD/GD entry and other police records

Police records can become important in theft, serious accidents, fire, suspected criminal activity, injuries or other circumstances where the incident has been reported to authorities.

Example: In a vehicle theft claim, the police report can establish the reported theft, vehicle details and circumstances. In a fire case, a police record may form part of the broader evidence file. The exact document needed depends on the facts and applicable process.

Do not assume that every minor motor accident requires an FIR. Follow the actual circumstances and applicable requirements.

07 • REPAIR

Repair estimate: a proposal, not automatically the final payable amount

A workshop estimate helps show what repairs the workshop proposes and at what cost. It does not by itself establish that every line item is covered or payable under the policy.

Look for

Part name, quantity, part price, labour, paint/materials, taxes and other charges.

Compare

Workshop estimate vs survey/assessment vs final invoice.

Question

Any major difference should be identified item-by-item rather than argued as one total.

08 • ASSESSMENT

Survey and assessment records

Keep the surveyor appointment information, inspection date, photographs, assessment/estimate documents provided to you, emails and written observations. If there is a disagreement about a part or repair method, record the precise item.

Useful comparison: “Rear bumper — workshop says replace; assessment allows repair.” That is more useful than “surveyor cut my claim.”
09 • FINAL REPAIR RECORD

Repair invoice and payment proof

The final invoice can show what work was actually performed and what was charged. Keep payment proof where you paid the workshop.

10 • SETTLEMENT

Settlement letter and payment record

If the insurer sends a settlement calculation, save it. It is often the best document for understanding why the final amount differs from the workshop estimate.

This is a learning template, not a universal calculation. The applicable policy and settlement method control.

11 • THE MOST UNDERRATED DOCUMENT

Build your own claim timeline

You do not need a complicated legal file. A one-page timeline can make a long claim much easier to understand.

Why this matters: A clear timeline prevents the claim from becoming a collection of disconnected phone calls.
12 • READY-TO-USE RECORDS

ClaimVoice templates

Document submission note

“Please find attached the documents listed below. Kindly acknowledge receipt and confirm whether any document remains outstanding.”

Status request

“Please confirm the present stage of claim no. ___, the specific item pending, whether any action is required from me, and the next expected step.”

Deduction clarification

“Please provide an item-wise calculation of the settlement, identifying depreciation, excess/deductible, non-covered items and any other material deduction.”

Rejection clarification

“Please provide the detailed written reason for the decision and identify the specific policy wording relied upon.”

13 • SAFETY

What you should NOT casually share

Never share passwordsInsurance portal, email, bank or other account passwords.
Never share OTPsAn OTP should not be given to a stranger claiming to process a claim.
Protect bank credentialsDo not disclose PINs, CVV, login credentials or full banking access details.
Limit public postingDo not upload complete IDs, policy documents or personal records publicly unless necessary.
Verify the recipientUse official insurer/workshop channels and verify where sensitive documents are going.
Ask whyIf a sensitive document is requested, ask its purpose, scope and relevance.

Documents are only useful when connected to the claim
